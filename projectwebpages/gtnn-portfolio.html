Background

What is Optimization in the Inverse Design of Nanophotonics?

Nanophotonic devices manipulate light at the nanoscale by controlling how electromagnetic waves interact with precisely shaped material structures. The behavior of such devices depends critically on their physical geometry, and this relationship is highly non-linear and unintuitive.

Forward design starts with a structure and asks: what does this do to light? This is relatively straightforward to simulate. Inverse design flips this entirely: given a desired optical behavior, what structure should we build? This is far harder. It is a high-dimensional search problem where many different structures can produce similar performance, and most of the design space produces poor results.

"The algorithmic design of nanophotonic structures promises to significantly improve the efficiency of nanophotonic components due to the strong dependence of electromagnetic function on geometry and the unintuitive connection between structure and response."

In the context of this work, the target devices are color splitters: nanostructures printed directly on top of camera sensor pixels that separate incoming light into red, green, and blue channels and redirect each to spatially distinct detector pixels. Unlike conventional color filter arrays, which absorb roughly two-thirds of incident light, these fully dielectric splitters can theoretically achieve photon transmission efficiencies exceeding 80%, making them highly attractive for next-generation digital imaging.

Optimization in this context means algorithmically searching the vast space of possible nanostructure geometries to find those that come closest to a target transmission spectrum. The challenge is that each candidate structure evaluation requires a full electromagnetic simulation, making exhaustive search computationally prohibitive.

Core Idea

The GTNN Approach: Four Key Innovations

The Generative Tandem Neural Network addresses the above challenges through a set of complementary innovations, each solving a distinct limitation of prior approaches.

01
🔀

Single Input, Multiple Output Tandem Network

A noise vector is injected alongside the target transmission spectrum into the inverse network. This transforms the traditional single-output tandem architecture into a generative model capable of producing many diverse structural candidates for the same spectral target. Each pass through the network yields a different valid solution. As far as the authors are aware, this approach had not been previously proposed for tandem networks.

02
🎲

Addressing the Non-Uniqueness Problem

In nanophotonic inverse design, many different physical geometries can produce nearly identical optical responses. By using a stochastic noise input, and with the help of pre-trained forward network the GTNN acknowledges and exploits this many-to-one mapping. Each sample from the network represents a plausible solution in this degenerate solution space, effectively making the network competitive with generative approaches like cGANs and VAEs for this task.

03
🧬

Extrapolation via Genetic Algorithm

The inverse network alone performs well within the training distribution but struggles with out-of-distribution target specifications. To overcome this, a genetic algorithm is applied to the input space of the inverse network. The GA evolves noise vectors toward inputs that, when passed through the frozen inverse and forward networks, yield the highest predicted performance, enabling genuine optimization beyond the training data.

04
🔁

Hybrid Model with Iterative Transfer Learning

Rather than requiring a large simulation dataset, the GTNN first trains on a broad, lower-fidelity dataset to learn the general structure-performance relationship, then refines using a small set of just 128 high-quality samples generated through adjoint topology optimization. This iterative transfer learning strategy dramatically reduces simulation cost while maintaining accuracy.

Together, these four ideas constitute a hybrid methodology that combines the speed of neural network inference, the diversity of generative modeling, and the optimization power of evolutionary algorithms in a framework trainable on a very small labeled dataset.

Methodology

How GTNN Works

The GTNN framework consists of three interconnected components that are designed and trained together to perform generative inverse design and optimization.

Forward Network — Predicting Optical Performance

The forward network takes a 2D binary nanostructure geometry as input and predicts its transmission spectrum across the red, green, and blue color channels. It is implemented as a CNN (ResNet50), designed to capture the spatial hierarchies of the device structure.

The network first trained on a very small dataset with 128 high quality adjoint optimized samples and afterward iteratively trained the network using new structures generated from the initial inverse network trained on the similar dataset. Once trained, it serves as a fast surrogate for full electromagnetic simulation. It performs well in both interpolation (within the training distribution) and extrapolation (beyond it), and acts as the fitness evaluator inside the genetic algorithm stage.

Architecture
CNN (ResNet50)
Input
2D binary nanostructure geometry
Output
Transmission spectra (R, G, B channels)
Training strategy
Transfer learning on 128 adjoint-optimized samples

Inverse Network — Generating Candidate Structures

The inverse network receives a target transmission spectrum and a random noise vector as inputs, producing a candidate structural geometry as output. It is built on a Convolutional Neural Network combined with the Forward Network within the tandem training framework.

During training, the output of the inverse network is fed through the frozen forward network, and the loss is computed on the predicted spectrum versus the target and binary cross entropy loss for the generated and existing structures. The noise vector is the key innovation: by sampling different noise vectors for the same target spectrum, the network produces structurally distinct yet high-performing designs, removing the major limitation of tandem networks.

Architecture
Convolutional Neural Network + Forward Network
Input
Target spectrum + noise vector z
Output
Diverse candidate structural geometries
Key innovation
SIMO: multiple valid structures per target spectrum

Genetic Algorithm — Optimization and Extrapolation

While the inverse network excels at interpolation within the training distribution, it struggles when the desired performance lies outside what the training data covers. A genetic algorithm is therefore applied to optimize the input vector to the inverse network.

A population of noise vectors is initialized randomly. Each is passed through the inverse and forward networks to obtain a predicted performance score using a figure of merit (FOM) based on selectivity and sensitivity. The GA then applies selection, crossover, and mutation to evolve the population. Because it navigates the learned input space rather than raw structure space, the search is far more efficient and enables designs that go beyond the training set.

Method
Genetic Algorithm on latent input space
Fitness function
FOM: Selectivity and Sensitivity
GA operations
Selection, Mutation, Crossover
Output
Input matrix to generate extrapolative structures
Results

Performance & Key Findings

The GTNN was evaluated on the task of designing 2D dielectric color splitter structures compatible with 800 nm sensor pixels, targeting specific red, green, and blue transmission spectra.

128
High-quality adjoint-optimized training samples, a remarkably small labeled dataset sufficient for the full model
SIMO
Single Input, Multiple Output: diverse structural candidates generated per target spectrum by varying the noise vector
GA+
Genetic Algorithm overcomes the inverse network's extrapolation limitation, achieving performance beyond the training set

Forward Network Accuracy

The forward network demonstrated strong performance in both interpolation (predictions on designs similar to training data) and extrapolation (predictions on designs outside the training distribution). Its accuracy as a surrogate model is essential since it serves inside the fitness function in the genetic algorithm, and inaccurate forward predictions would mislead the search entirely.

Inverse Network — Interpolation

For target spectra within the training distribution, the inverse network successfully generated valid and high-performing structural geometries. The noise vector input allowed exploration of the non-unique solution space, producing a range of structurally distinct designs that all approached the target performance.

Inverse Network — Extrapolation and GA Fix

When tasked with target spectra significantly outside the training distribution, the inverse network alone showed degraded performance. Applying the genetic algorithm to obtain the input matrix that can produce extrapolative structures, successfully recovered high-quality results, producing structures with performance competitive with or exceeding those in the training set.

Outperforming Topology Optimization

The full GTNN pipeline achieved significant improvement in nanophotonic structure performance compared to traditional gradient-based topology optimization, even though the training data was itself generated using that same optimizer. This demonstrates the framework's ability to synthesize and surpass its own training baseline.

Key Takeaway

The GTNN represents a significant advance in neural-network-based inverse design of nanophotonic devices. By combining a generative single-input multiple-output tandem architecture with iterative transfer learning and genetic algorithm optimization, the framework overcomes the three core limitations of prior approaches: lack of output diversity, poor extrapolation, and high data requirements, all within a single unified pipeline. The method not only achieves high-quality interpolative design but also enables genuine optimization beyond the training set, outperforming the simulation-based optimizer that generated its own training data.

This hybrid methodology represents an important shift: rather than treating neural networks as simple function approximators for inverse design, GTNN positions the trained network as the foundation for a generative optimization engine. The forward network becomes a fast, learned simulator; the inverse network becomes a constrained generator; and the genetic algorithm becomes a guided explorer of the learned design space.

Future Directions

What's Next

This work opens several promising directions for future research and real-world application.

DIRECTION 01

Extension to 3D Nanophotonic Structures

The GTNN framework can be extended to multi-dimensional nanophotonic color splitter structures.

DIRECTION 02

Broader Nanophotonic Applications

The generality of the approach can be validated by applying it to other inverse design problems beyond color splitters, including broadband lenses, metasurfaces, and wavelength-division multiplexers. Each represents a distinct design challenge that would test the robustness and flexibility of the full GTNN pipeline.

DIRECTION 03

Integration with Fabrication Constraints

Future work can incorporate fabrication constraints directly into the optimization process to ensure that generated structures are not only optically optimal but also manufacturable with existing lithographic processes. This is the key step toward real-world deployment in next-generation imaging sensors.
