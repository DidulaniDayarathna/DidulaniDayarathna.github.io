Background

The Nanophotonic Inverse Design Problem

Nanophotonic structures manipulate light at the subwavelength scale. Their optical properties depend critically on geometry in a highly non-linear and counter-intuitive way. While forward design (predicting optical response from a known structure) can be simulated, inverse design is far harder: given a desired spectrum, what geometry produces it?

Traditional simulation-based approaches such as adjoint topology optimization are computationally expensive, can get trapped in local optima, and do not scale well. Machine learning offers a potential solution: networks trained on simulated data can predict optical responses orders of magnitude faster than FDTD simulation, and can also be configured to generate structures from desired spectra directly.

This paper systematically explores four forward networks and five inverse networks using diverse ML architectures, comparing their performance at interpolation (within the training distribution) and extrapolation (beyond the training distribution), revealing that good interpolation does not guarantee good extrapolation.

Interpolation vs. Extrapolation — Conceptual Illustration
1 💻

Computational Cost

Full FDTD electromagnetic simulation of each candidate structure is prohibitively slow for large-scale search. Neural networks reduce inference time by orders of magnitude.

2 🎯

No Global Optimum Guarantee

Gradient-based simulation methods like adjoint optimization are sensitive to initialization and can converge to local optima rather than globally optimal structures.

3 📐

Interpolation vs. Extrapolation Gap

Networks that perform well on target spectra within their training distribution often fail dramatically on out-of-distribution targets, a critical and understudied limitation.

Forward Design

Four Approaches to Forward Modeling

Forward modeling predicts the optical response (absorption spectrum) from an input structural geometry. Four fundamentally different architectures were implemented and compared. The goal is to find the fastest, most accurate surrogate for full FDTD simulation.

Forward Network Task: Structure → Spectrum

ResNet50: Pretrained Transfer Learning

A ResNet50 architecture pretrained on ImageNet is fine-tuned on the nanophotonic dataset. This leverages the rich spatial feature representations already learned from millions of natural images to predict optical spectra from binary nanostructure images.

Transfer learning dramatically reduces training time, as most weights are already well-initialized. The model demonstrates strong performance in both interpolation and extrapolation, making it the most robust forward network tested. Very little additional training time is required beyond fine-tuning.

Architecture
ResNet50 (50-layer residual CNN)
Pre-training
ImageNet (1M+ natural images)
Strength
Best extrapolation performance among forward networks
Key Advantage
Little additional training needed; robust spatial feature transfer

DNN: Deep Fully-Connected Network

A traditional deep neural network with multiple fully-connected hidden layers. The 40×40 binary input matrix is flattened to a 1600-dimensional vector and passed through successive dense layers to predict the spectrum.

The DNN provides a clean baseline. Despite its simplicity, it performs competitively at interpolation and, importantly, outperforms more complex architectures at extrapolation. This counterintuitive result highlights the risk of overfitting spatial inductive biases to the training distribution.

Architecture
Fully-connected dense layers
Input
Flattened 1600-dim binary vector
Strength
Surprisingly strong extrapolation performance
Key Finding
Simpler networks can generalize better to out-of-distribution samples

CNN: Convolutional Neural Network

A convolutional neural network that applies learned spatial filters across the 40×40 input grid. Convolutional layers are designed to capture spatial hierarchies and local patterns, including silicon region shapes and their arrangements.

The CNN generally performs well at interpolation, benefiting from its ability to learn structure-specific spatial features. However, its extrapolation performance is worse than the simple DNN.

Architecture
Convolutional layers + pooling + FC head
Input
40×40 binary image (spatial structure)
Strength
Interpolation accuracy; spatial feature learning
Weakness
Extrapolation suffers vs. simpler DNN

PCA + DNN: Classical ML + Neural Network Hybrid

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) first reduces the 1600-dimensional binary structure to a compact set of principal components. These compressed representations are then fed into a DNN for spectrum prediction.

This combined approach achieves the lowest training loss and fastest convergence at interpolation, since PCA effectively removes noise and redundancy in the binary matrix. However, the dimensionality reduction may lose information critical for extrapolative generalization, leading to weaker out-of-distribution performance.

Architecture
PCA dimensionality reduction → DNN
Compression
1600-dim → compact PCA components
Strength
Fastest convergence, lowest interpolation loss
Weakness
Reduced extrapolation performance
Inverse Design

Five Approaches to Inverse Design

Inverse design generates candidate nanostructures from a desired target spectrum. This is significantly harder than forward modeling: the problem is non-unique (many structures can produce similar spectra), and the output is a high-dimensional binary image rather than a smooth spectrum curve. Five different inverse architectures were implemented.

Inverse Network Task: Target Spectrum → Generated Structures

Inverse DNN: Fully-Connected for Inverse Design

A fully-connected deep neural network takes the target absorption spectrum as input and generates a 40×40 binary nanostructure directly. A noise vector is concatenated to the spectrum input, enabling the network to generate diverse candidate structures for the same target.

The DNN inverse network is the top performer for extrapolation among all five inverse networks. Its simplicity avoids overfitting spatial biases to the training distribution, allowing more flexible structure generation. This result mirrors the forward network finding: simpler architectures can generalize better.

Architecture
Fully-connected layers; spectrum + noise → structure
Training
Spectrum + noise vector → predicted structure → evaluate via FDTD labels
Key Result
Best extrapolation performance among all five inverse networks
Noise Role
Enables SIMO: multiple distinct structures per target spectrum

Inverse CNN (Original Labels): Convolutional Inverse

A CNN-based inverse network trained directly with the original FDTD-generated spectra as ground-truth labels. The network must learn to decode a spectrum signal into a spatially structured 40×40 binary image.

CNN(Org) performs reasonably well at interpolation tasks. However, without filtering the labels through the forward network, there is a mismatch between the label spectra and the visual spatial structure the CNN learns to decode. Its extrapolation performance is moderate.

Architecture
Transposed/deconvolution CNN
Labels
Original FDTD simulation spectra
Strength
Good spatial structure generation at interpolation
Limitation
Direct FDTD labels may not align with CNN-learned representations

Inverse CNN (Forward Filter): Tandem Architecture

The CNN inverse network is trained in a tandem configuration: the generated structure is passed through a frozen pre-trained forward CNN, and the composite loss is computed on the forward network's predicted spectrum rather than the raw FDTD label.

This tandem training strategy improves consistency between the inverse network's output and the physical spectrum objective. It addresses the non-uniqueness problem by closing the forward-inverse loop. CNN(Fwd) shows improved consistency over CNN(Org) particularly for interpolative structures.

Architecture
CNN inverse + frozen forward CNN (tandem)
Loss
Spectrum loss computed via forward network prediction
Strength
Better spectrum consistency; handles non-uniqueness
Design Pattern
Forward-inverse tandem; a foundational approach in nanophotonic ML

Autoencoder + DNN: Latent Space Design

An autoencoder first learns a compact latent representation of the nanostructure space from all training structures. The DNN then learns to map from an input spectrum to this learned latent space. The decoder reconstructs the final structure from the latent code.

The autoencoder enforces a smooth, regularized latent structure representation. However, this two-stage approach shows weaker extrapolation performance than the simpler DNN, suggesting the latent space learned from training structures does not generalize well to novel out-of-distribution targets.

Architecture
Autoencoder (encoder + decoder) + separate DNN mapping
Stage 1
Train autoencoder on all structures → compressed latent code
Stage 2
DNN learns: target spectrum → latent code → decoder → structure
Limitation
Extrapolation limited by training-distribution latent space

Inverse PCA + DNN: Classical Compression Route

PCA decomposes all training structures into principal components. The DNN learns to predict the PCA coefficients from an input spectrum. The inverse PCA transform then reconstructs the candidate structure from those predicted coefficients.

PCA + DNN provides the fastest training time due to the small number of parameters in the DNN when operating in compressed PCA space. However, the extrapolation performance suffers relative to the DNN: PCA components fitted on the training set don't span the structure space needed for out-of-distribution generation.

Architecture
PCA of training structures + DNN + inverse PCA
Speed
Fastest training among all five inverse networks
Strength
Compact representation; very low parameter count
Limitation
Extrapolation capability limited by training-distribution PCA basis
Results & Key Findings

Key Finding: Interpolation ≠ Extrapolation

Networks that achieve the lowest loss on in-distribution validation data do not necessarily perform best on out-of-distribution targets. PCA+DNN excels at interpolation but struggles with extrapolation, while the simple DNN shows the opposite trade-off.

Transfer Learning Bridges the Gap

ResNet50 achieves strong performance in both regimes by leveraging general visual features learned from ImageNet. Pretrained representations encode universal spatial concepts that transfer well beyond the training distribution of nanostructure geometries.

Simple Beats Complex for Extrapolation

In both forward and inverse design tasks, fully-connected DNNs outperform more architecturally complex models (CNNs, autoencoders) at extrapolation.

Successful Out-of-Distribution Generation

The inverse networks, particularly the DNN, were able to generate structures with spectral responses significantly outside the training distribution when using noise vector sampling, demonstrating genuine generative capability beyond simple interpolation.

Generative Capability

Noise Vectors & Diverse Structure Generation

A key capability explored in this work is the use of random noise vectors to generate structurally diverse candidates from a single target spectrum. By sampling different noise vectors and passing each through the inverse network, the model produces multiple structurally distinct geometries, all targeting the same desired optical response.

SIMO Generative Sampling — One Spectrum, Many Structures

For each test spectrum (both interpolative and extrapolative), 10 different noise vectors were sampled and 10 candidate structures generated. Performance was assessed both on average MSE across all 10, and on the best-performing structure (minimum error), to capture both consistency and peak capability of each network.

Core Conclusion

Different machine learning architectures show fundamentally different aptitudes for interpolation versus extrapolation in nanophotonic inverse design. Combining classical ML (PCA) with ANNs achieves strong interpolation but weak extrapolation; transfer learning (ResNet50) bridges the gap most effectively. Critically, simple fully-connected DNNs outperform structurally complex architectures at extrapolation, challenging the intuition that architectural complexity always leads to better generalization. These networks can successfully generate structures with optical responses well outside their training distribution, demonstrating genuine ML-driven extrapolative design in nanophotonics.

This work positions ML not merely as an efficient proxy for simulation, but as a genuine design engine capable of exploring structure-performance spaces beyond what the training data directly covers. The systematic comparison across architectures provides practical guidance for researchers building ML pipelines for nanophotonic and broader photonic inverse design systems.

Future Directions

What's Next

This work lays a systematic empirical foundation and opens several high-impact directions for future research.

DIRECTION 01

Generative Tandem Networks with Noise Injection

Building directly on this work, the GTNN framework (ACS Omega / Physica Scripta 2025) combines a noise-injected tandem inverse network with iterative transfer learning and a genetic algorithm, achieving diverse structure generation and extrapolative optimization simultaneously.

DIRECTION 02

3D and Multi-Dimensional Structures

Extending these ML architectures to three-dimensional nanostructures and multi-layer devices would dramatically expand the design space, enabling components like volumetric color splitters, metasurfaces, and broadband nanophotonic lenses.

DIRECTION 03

Physics-Informed and Hybrid Approaches

Incorporating physical constraints directly into network training, such as symmetry priors, Maxwell's equations, or fabrication constraints, could help close the interpolation–extrapolation performance gap while ensuring generated structures are physically and practically realizable.
