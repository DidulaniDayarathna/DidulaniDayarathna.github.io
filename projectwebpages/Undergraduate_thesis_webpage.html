Undergraduate Thesis — University of Colombo

Development of a Combinatorial Optical Property Measuring System for Classification & Quantification of Liquids

AuthorS.A.D.A. Dayarathna
DegreeBSc Special — Engineering Physics
UniversityUniversity of Colombo, Sri Lanka
YearDecember 2018
SupervisorDr. Hiran H.E. Jayaweera
A non-destructive optical property measuring system capable of measuring transmittance, absorption, refraction, and scattering of liquids was designed and constructed. Using LEDs spanning 375–850 nm, Hamamatsu photodiodes with transimpedance amplifiers, and a 48 kS/s DAQ system with a quartz tube sample holder, the device takes a full measurement within 15 seconds. Multivariate analysis (SVD) was used for data compression. Machine learning — supervised (SVD matrix model), unsupervised (PCA, hierarchical clustering), and an artificial neural network — was applied to quantify and classify liquids. Tests were conducted on KMnO₄, CuSO₄, Co(CH₃COO)₂·4H₂O, K₂Cr₂O₇ (non-scattering) and milk (scattering), achieving classification specificity and precision of 1.0 for all five liquids, with neural network validation accuracy of 91.86%.

01 — Introduction

Background & Objective

A combinatorial, non-destructive optical measurement device for identifying and classifying liquid samples using machine learning techniques.

Measurements are one of the most fundamental concepts in science, used to determine properties of an object by comparing them to a standard. Optical measurements are particularly advantageous as they enable non-destructive, non-invasive, high-accuracy, and near-instant measurements with machine vision. Applications range from monitoring fertilizer concentrations in agriculture to assessing water purity.

The key challenge addressed by this project is that optical properties of liquids — transmittance, absorption, refraction, and nephelometric scattering — are interdependent. Individual measurements alone cannot reliably quantify or classify liquids; a combinatorial approach is required. The system was therefore designed to simultaneously acquire all four optical properties in a single scan.

01.1 — Objective

Project Goal

The main objective was to use LEDs of different wavelengths (in the visible region) together with photodetectors to measure combinatorial optical properties — transmittance, nephelometric scattering, and absorption — simultaneously. Since these properties are interdependent and cannot be measured in isolation, a combined instrument was developed and trained using data compression and multivariate analysis techniques to classify and quantify liquid samples.

Key insight: Because optical properties of liquids are interdependent, measuring them individually is insufficient. This system combines all measurements in a single instrument and uses machine learning to extract useful information from the combined data.
02 — Instrument

Construction of the Optical System

A custom-built aluminium-housed instrument integrating LED arrays, photodiode arrays, transimpedance amplifiers, and a DAQ card — all controlled via software.

Optical Design
Hardware Layout
A quartz tube was used as the sample holder due to its excellent transmittance across both UV and visible spectral regions (unlike glass, which is limited to the visible range). An aluminium tube was fitted around it to block ambient light. Holes were drilled at 90° and 180° to mount LEDs and photodiodes for transmittance and nephelometric scattering detection simultaneously.
  • Two LED arrays per wavelength, mounted normal to the emission axis
  • Hamamatsu S5971 photodiodes — large active area, high quantum efficiency, wide spectral response
  • 90° array: captures nephelometric (side) scattering
  • 180° array: captures transmitted light
  • Eight photodiodes total, all activated for each LED scan
Electronic Design
Signal Chain
LEDs were driven with constant-current sources to maintain stable output power, grouped into 20 mA, 30 mA, and 40 mA categories. An Arduino Nano microcontroller switched LEDs one by one using C828 BJT transistors. Photodiode photocurrents were converted to voltages via transimpedance amplifiers, then digitised by a 14-bit ADC in the NI-USB 6009 DAQ card at up to 48 kS/s.
  • Current drivers used (not voltage) to avoid thermal runaway in LEDs
  • Transimpedance amplifier: current → usable voltage, more linear response
  • 14-bit ADC for high-resolution digitisation
  • Firmware on Arduino controls LED sequencing via COM port commands
  • Data saved in binary files after each complete scan
02.1

Eleven 5 mm standard LEDs from Roithner Laser were used, spanning 375 nm to 810 nm. LEDs were selected because their ~30 nm full-width at half maximum (FWHM) makes them well-suited for liquids that absorb broad ranges of wavelengths, unlike sharp-line lasers. They are also low-cost and produce high-quality output.

04 — Methodology

Data Acquisition & Analysis Pipeline

A multi-stage pipeline: raw optical data → SVD compression → supervised & unsupervised machine learning → quantification and classification.

01

Data Acquisition

Each LED in both arrays is activated sequentially by the Arduino microcontroller. All eight photodiodes are active simultaneously, capturing both transmitted (180°) and scattered (90°) light. The DAQ card records voltage outputs from each photodiode at 48 kS/s, producing a large dataset for every sample. One complete scan takes 15 seconds per sample.

Hardware Phase
02

Data Compression — Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

Due to the high sampling rate, each sample produces a very large dataset. SVD factorises the data matrix A into three matrices: A = U S Vᵀ. The diagonal elements of S (singular values) are ranked by significance. Only the highest-significance principal components (typically 5–7) are retained, capturing ≥80% of the data while discarding noise. The truncated left singular vectors (U) are then used for modelling.

Unsupervised — Dimensionality Reduction
03a

Concentration Quantification — Supervised Matrix Model

A model matrix A is derived from known concentration data (expert matrix E) using the relation E = U × A, solved as A = E · U⁻¹. Leave-one-out cross-validation is applied: for each of n samples, the model is rebuilt without that sample, then used to predict its concentration. Predicted values are plotted against real values; correlation coefficients and residuals are computed to assess fit quality.

Supervised Machine Learning
03b

Classification — Approach I: Statistical Clustering (PCA + Hierarchical)

All liquid datasets are merged and compressed together via SVD. The first three principal components are plotted in a 3D scatter plot to visualise natural clusters. Additionally, a hierarchical clustering dendrogram is generated using pairwise Euclidean distances (MATLAB's pdist and linkage functions), grouping samples by similarity. Confusion matrices are then computed for each liquid to quantify specificity, sensitivity, accuracy, and precision.

Unsupervised Machine Learning
03c

Classification — Approach II: Artificial Neural Network

A multi-layer neural network was trained using 70% training / 15% validation / 15% test data splits. Both transmittance (180°) and scattering (90°) data were combined into 16 input features. Data was arranged in overlapping blocks of 32 rows. Six network architectures were compared. The best architecture used two hidden layers of 512 nodes each, with dropout regularisation, ReLU activation, and a softmax output layer. A data generator was used to manage the large dataset without exceeding RAM.

Deep Learning
04

Spectrometric Analysis

An Ocean Optics Flame USB 4000 spectrometer was used separately to record absorption and transmittance spectra for each liquid. Measurements were taken with a halogen lamp inside a light-sealed box, with the sample beaker positioned between lamp and spectrometer. This enabled identification of peak transmittance wavelengths for each liquid, which were compared with the LED wavelengths used in the main instrument.

06 — Classification

Liquid Classification Results

Two approaches were evaluated for classifying all five liquids simultaneously. Both correctly identified all five liquid groups from 169 total samples.

The combined dataset of 169 samples (all five liquids) was processed together for classification. After SVD-based dimensionality reduction, two distinct classification approaches were applied and compared.

Approach I — Statistical
PCA + Hierarchical Clustering
The first three principal components of the left singular vector were plotted in a 3D scatter plot (PCA). Five distinct clusters were visible, each corresponding to one liquid. K₂Cr₂O₇ showed a slightly wider spread due to outliers.
  • PCA 3D plot clearly separated all five liquids into distinct clusters
  • Hierarchical dendrogram (pdist + linkage) confirmed the same five clusters plus outlier/false-negative branches
  • Scattering (milk) and non-scattering liquids were successfully distinguished from each other
  • Processing time: much faster than neural network approach
  • Result: simple, quick, and highly accurate — recommended primary method
Approach II — Deep Learning
Artificial Neural Network
A multi-layer neural network was trained on 16 input features (8 transmittance + 8 scattering photodiode readings). Data was structured into overlapping 32-row blocks. Six architectures were compared. The best model achieved 91.86% validation accuracy.
  • Architecture: 1 input layer + 2 hidden layers (512 nodes each, dropout) + 1 softmax output
  • Activation: ReLU (hidden layers), Softmax (output)
  • Training time: ~22 hours; evaluation: ~3 hours
  • Validation accuracy: 91.86% (best of 6 tested architectures)
  • Conclusion: less efficient than statistical approach for this task

07 — Conclusion

Discussion & Conclusions

The combinatorial optical property measuring system was successfully designed, constructed, and validated. It delivers non-destructive, non-invasive, high-accuracy, and near-instant measurements with machine vision — a full sample scan completed in just 15 seconds.

Three reasons drove the need for combinatorial measurements: (1) light propagates through the quartz tube in complex paths, requiring a full photodiode array to capture all rays; (2) a photodiode array enables simultaneous detection of scattered and transmitted light; (3) Rayleigh scattering affects both short and long wavelengths differently, and the multi-detector layout captures both regimes.

For concentration quantification, all four non-scattering liquids yielded correlation coefficients above 0.85 (with KMnO₄ and CuSO₄ at 0.99), and the scattering liquid milk achieved r = 0.87 — demonstrating strong predictive power across both liquid types. All residual plots were randomly distributed around zero, confirming the appropriateness of linear models.

For classification, both PCA/hierarchical clustering and the neural network successfully separated all five liquids. The statistical method was faster, simpler, and comparably accurate (specificity and precision = 1.0 for all liquids; milk achieved 100% on all four metrics), while the neural network required ~22 hours to train and achieved 91.86% validation accuracy. The statistical approach is therefore preferred for this application.

The device also demonstrated the ability to classify liquids when mixed with a base solution, distinguishing neutral KMnO₄ from KMnO₄+NaOH mixtures with 95% accuracy and 100% specificity — a capability with direct practical relevance.

07.1 — Future Work

Further Implementation

🔬 Inelastic Optical Properties (Fluorescence)

The quartz sample holder was deliberately chosen because it transmits UV wavelengths (unlike glass), enabling future measurement of fluorescence — an inelastic optical property where wavelength and intensity change. Voltage-controlled current drivers would be needed to maintain constant power over time for inelastic measurements.

📐 Improved Scattering Quantification

Beer-Lambert law cannot be directly applied to scattering liquids because the effective light path length is unknown (it exceeds the physical cuvette width due to scattering events). Further work is needed to develop a more accurate quantification model for scattering liquids beyond the SVD matrix approach.

🏭 Industrial Quality Control

The system is well-suited for automated liquid quality control in manufacturing environments — for example, monitoring soft drinks or beverages on production lines without operator involvement. The device can detect deviations from a trained liquid profile even when the contaminant is present in small quantities.

📱 Miniaturisation & Mobile Interface

The system could be miniaturised using a single-board computer such as a Raspberry Pi Zero, with a companion mobile application to control the device and acquire data remotely — eliminating the need for a desktop computer and large display. This would enable portable field deployment.
